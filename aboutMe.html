Software Engineer in Beta

I love to rock climb, and I love to solve problems through code. In both disciplines there is a term called "beta". In rock climbing, the phrase "giving beta" means that you are giving the person climbing tips and tricks from your vantage point on the ground. In software development, a product in beta means it is still in development. I believe both definitions currently apply to me. I am both learning a route where the path isn't always clear, and my skills are a work in progress.

Getting beta on a climb can be challenging, because you place a lot of trust in the person that is giving you the advice. As the climber, you are so close to the problem that you can't always see the way forward. To help move my skills in software development forward, I am putting my trust in a nonprofit organization called LaunchCode.

Becoming a lifelong learner

When I taught fourth grade, a common struggle I faced was when a student recognized they made a mistake on an assignment and became unwilling to try anything to fix the problem. Students would become frustrated or mad at themselves and be unable to imagine a future where they get the answers correct. This was most common in math class, and it is a common struggle for elementary school children because they do not yet have a fully developed prefrontal cortex. The prefrontal cortex helps manage executive functions like emotional regulation, planning, and impulse control. To help my students overcome their frustrations and give them tools to use in the future when they become frustrated, I taught them skills from the book “Mindset: The New Psychology of Success” By Carol Dweck. In her research, Dweck found that something she termed a “Growth Mindset” helped people see mistakes in a new light; not as something to be hidden and looked down upon, but something to be valued. The general idea is that when you make a mistake like on a math assignment, you know now what you do not understand. If you are in elementary school, you can tell your teacher or parent and he or she will work with you to help you understand. If you are an adult learning a new programming language, you can post for help on Stack, Github, or any variety of places. You can go to MDN to read information on the problems you’re facing. The Growth Mindset is knowing how to ask for help because you made a mistake and want to get better. For my fourth-grade students, I turned all of this research into an easily digestible mantra they could repeat when they were frustrated: I don’t understand this…. Yet. The statement works because it recognizes the frustration you are feeling while the yet emphasizes that learning is a process. “Yet” makes all the difference, as it shows that the ability will develop. Believing that you can get better at something promotes resilience, reduces the fear of failure, and encourages a lifelong learning mindset. I now use the same mantra for myself. When I am frustrated or confused I give myself the grace to acknowledge the point of consternation and then I make myself add the word “yet.” The beauty in using this is that you truly become a lifelong learner. “Yet” refuses to let you become set in your ways, because if you don’t understand something it’s only because you don’t understand something yet. You will ask for help and sooner than later, you will understand.

I am TRULY a lifelong learner.
